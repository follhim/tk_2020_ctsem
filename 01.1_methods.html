In this section, I (attempt) to unpack our analytical approach and how to interpret results. When it comes to interpretting results, I will be pulling from our analytical models, so you should probably review the latter chapters before reading that section here.

I’m also writing this because this is likely what I would write up in a paper too (although we would likely condense below as it is a bit too detailed).

What are DT-models?

To understand how continuous-time models work, we begin with discrete-time models.

Note

Imagine a simple ESM study, we might have 150 adults, and track (positive and negative) affect 5 times a day across 8 days. For the sake of simplicity, lets assume these beeps are sent out at a specific time (8 am, 10 am, 12pm, 2pm, 5pm).

Aside from 2-5pm (3hr jump) and 5p-8am (15hr jump), time-intervals are 2 hrs.

In a classic multi-level model (MLM), you would cluster around the person to account for non-independence of “beep” observations and perform person-mean centering1 to parse out within-person associations between positive and negative affect. In a typical VAR model (e.g., VAR-DSEM) we would estimate:

  1. The covariance between NAw (within-person) and PAw at t-1 and at t.
  2. The auto-regressive effects between NAwt-1 and NAwt, and between PAwt-1 and PAwt-1.
  3. Finally, the cross-lagged effects from NAwt-1 on PAwt and PAwt-1 on NAwt.

So the main outputs here are the regressive effects: AR and CL.

In a matrix format, you might present then as

\[ \boldsymbol{\eta}_{t} = \mathbf{c} + \boldsymbol{\Phi}\,\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t-1} + \boldsymbol{\zeta}_t, \qquad \boldsymbol{\zeta}_t \sim N(\mathbf{0}, \boldsymbol{\Psi}) \tag{1}\]

\[ \boldsymbol{\eta}_t = \begin{bmatrix} \text{NA}^{w}_{t} \\ \text{PA}^{w}_{t} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\Phi} = \begin{bmatrix} \phi_{NN} & \phi_{NP} \\ \phi_{PN} & \phi_{PP} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\Psi} = \begin{bmatrix} \psi_{NN} & \psi_{NP} \\ \psi_{PN} & \psi_{PP} \end{bmatrix} \tag{2}\]

That’s a lof of equations, but essentially this is the formula used to derive our regression parameters. The main output we are interested is the middle column Equation 2. The diagonal elements are the auto-regressive effects. So \(\phi_{NN}\) represents the AR(1) effect of NAwt-1 and NAwt. The off-diagonal elements are the cross-lagged effects so \(\phi_{NP}\) represents CL(1) of NAwt-1 on PAwt.

The problem with DT-models

Irregular Time-Intervals

The first problem with DT-models are that they not only assume but require equal intervals between each beep. Having 3hr or 15hr intervals mixed in violates key assumptions of DT-models. This presents problem both for deriving our parameters of interest, but also for night-effects. Many choose to move forward with this assumption or leverage other approaches model effects such that the 5pm–>8am response is coded as missing or removed altogether. An assumption simulation studies show is highly unlikely.

Non-comparability across studies

The ability to compare temporal dynamics between different studies is practically impossible given lagged effects are entirely determined by time interval (you couldn’t compare lagged effects from 2hr beeps to 5hr beeps studies). And that’s even if the underlying temporal dynamics are the same.

Lag-dependence of cross-effects

The cross-lagged effects can rise, peak, and decay all as a function of change in time. Conclusions like “NA predicts PA more strongly than PA predicts NA” can reverse depending on the interval (Kuiper & Ryan, 2018; Driver, 2025).

Lag-dependence of dynamic effects

A DT-VAR returns one autoregressive and one cross-lagged parameter per pair of variables. It is tempting to read these as the effect of NA on itself, or of PA on NA. They are more accurately read as the effect at the specific interval the study happened to use. Under a CT model, every element of \(\boldsymbol{\Phi}\) is a function of the interval, so each DT parameter is a single point on a curve.

Auto Effects

In a DT model, \(\phi_{NN}\) tells us how much of a deviation in NA remains after one lag. What it cannot tell us is how that deviation dissipates. We cannot say how quickly NA returns halfway to baseline, whether most of the recovery happens in the first hour or is spread across the day, or what the carry-over would be at any interval other than the one we measured. In principle, \(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^k\) gives the implied carry-over \(k\) lags ahead, but only at multiples of the design interval, and only if that interval is constant. In our ESM design it is not: \(\phi_{NN}\) is forced to describe both the 2-hour daytime gaps and the 15-hour overnight gap with one number.

In CT, the auto-effect \(a_{NN}\) describes the underlying rate of return to equilibrium, from which the full decay curve \(\phi_{NN}(\Delta t)\) can be recovered for any interval. This gives interpretable quantities such as a half-life (?@eq-halflife), e.g., “a deviation in NA is halved after roughly \(X\) hours.”

In the bivariate case, even \(\phi_{NN}(\Delta t)\) is not purely a self-regulation effect. A shock to NA also shifts PA, which feeds back to NA through the cross-effects. So the DT autoregression conflates NA’s own regulation with this feedback loop, and the half-life is best computed numerically from the full \(\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\Delta t)\) rather than from \(a_{NN}\) alone.

Cross-Effects

The problem is sharper for cross-effects, because their curves are not monotonic:

  1. At \(\Delta t = 0\) the cross-effect is exactly zero, since \(\boldsymbol{\Phi}(0) = \mathbf{I}\). PA needs time to move NA.
  2. The effect then builds as PA’s influence on the rate of change in NA accumulates.
  3. It peaks at some interval and then decays toward zero, as both variables regulate back toward equilibrium.

This has three consequences:

  • Magnitude depends on the interval. The same process yields a larger cross-lag at the peak interval than at shorter or longer ones. A small DT cross-lag may reflect a weak effect, or a strong effect measured at the wrong interval.
  • Comparisons can reverse. \(\phi_{NP}(\Delta t)\) and \(\phi_{PN}(\Delta t)\) peak at different intervals, so claims such as “PA predicts NA more strongly than NA predicts PA” can flip depending on the interval chosen (Kuiper & Ryan, 2018).
  • Signs can reverse. With feedback between the variables, a cross-effect can be positive at short intervals and negative at longer ones (or vice versa), so even the direction of a DT cross-lag is not guaranteed to generalize.

Studies using different sampling schemes (e.g., 2-hour beeps vs. daily diaries) are therefore not estimating the same quantity, even when they study the same process. The CT drift matrix provides a common, interval-free reference point, and the full set of lag-specific effects can be recovered and reported for any interval of interest.
